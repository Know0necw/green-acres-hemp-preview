Hemp

A powerful plant.

Hemp is a commonly used term for high-growing varieties of the Cannabis plant and its products, which include fiber, oil, and seed. Hemp is refined into hemp-seed foods, hemp oil, wax, resin, rope, cloth, pulp, paper, and fuel.

Other variants of the herb Cannabis are widely used as a drug, commonly known as marijuana. Those variants are typically low-growing and have higher tetrahydrocannabinol (THC). Hemp is a cousin of the marijuana plant but contains little or no THC. Regulatory limits — particularly for THC — require strains bred for low THC content, 0.3% or lower.

Industrial hemp was outlawed in the 1930s in the United States, and was mandatory for farmers to grow in the Midwest, if you owned a farm, for the demand for rope for the war. Hemp was processed back in the early 1900s for sails, ships, rope, clothing, and many other things. The Declaration of Independence was written on hemp paper, as was the first Bible.

Industrial hemp was legalized in 14 states in 2014 and is grown for building houses, biodiesel fuel, clothing, medicines, and beauty products. Automakers are making interior parts such as door panels out of hemp. Bast fibers can be used in 100% hemp products, or blended with flax, cotton, or silk — most commonly a 55/45 hemp/cotton blend. The inner woody fibers go into mulch, animal bedding, and other industrial uses. The oil from the seeds oxidizes in air, like linseed oil, and is used in paints, creams, cooking, and plastics.

In modern times hemp is used for paper, textiles, clothing, biodegradable plastics, construction (hempcrete and insulation), body products, health food, and biofuel.
